# Create Demo

Help people see what changed and why it matters to them. Choose the smallest demonstration that makes that clear, then create the materials to deliver it.

## Establish the brief

Use the conversation first. Ask for any missing essentials together, without re-asking what is known:

- **Work:** What are we demonstrating? Get enough source material to understand the problem and outcome.
- **Audience:** Who is it for, and what do they already know?
- **Format:** Live walkthrough, narrated recording, slides, document, message/post, or a combination? Will someone talk it through, or must it stand alone?
- **Time:** How long is available? Clarify whether this includes questions. For an untimed post or document, agree a reading/viewing budget.

Wait for missing essentials before drafting the demo. If the user is unsure of the format, recommend the simplest one that shows the work and get their choice. Ask what collateral they want if the format does not establish that.

## Choose what earns attention

Define one audience takeaway: what should they understand, be able to do, or decide afterwards? Infer it where clear; ask if it would materially change the demo.

Recommend the strongest one to three proof points, fewer for short slots. For each, identify the visible evidence and why this audience cares. Prefer one realistic scenario or a before/after over a tour of every feature. Work without an interface can use an output, comparison, physical result, or measured change.

Cut implementation history, secondary features, and presenter achievements unless they change the audience's understanding. Keep detail available for questions. State material limitations after the high-level outcome, before anyone could draw a misleading conclusion. Distinguish working behaviour, prototypes, and future plans; never invent results, metrics, or effort.

## Build the story around the visuals

Use this sequence, compressed to suit the medium:

1. **Hook:** Open with a relevant question, surprising fact, provocative statement, or natural joke. Connect it directly to the problem. Avoid forced humour, clickbait, or an unrelated warm-up.
2. **Problem:** Show the friction and why this audience should care. Keep this brief enough to reach the demonstration early.
3. **Effort:** Give one short, factual line, such as “After two weeks and 30 iterations…”. No development diary. Ask for the fact if useful and unknown; omit it if unavailable.
4. **Demonstration:** Show the thing or outcome. Talk with the visual: orient attention, show the action or evidence, explain the consequence. Spend most of the time here. End on the visible result and its meaning; add a next step only if useful.

Make a compact storyboard pairing each visual/action with its spoken point and time allowance. For a post or document, pair visuals with brief captions instead. Merge beats where natural; this sequence does not require four slides.

## Make it shorter than the slot

As a practical default, target roughly 75% of the presentation time remaining after any explicit Q&A allowance. A five-minute demo should initially aim for about 3:45. This buffer is a planning heuristic, not a research finding.

Include clicks, loading, transitions, audience responses, and pauses to look. Rehearse with the actual visuals, or mark timing as estimated until the presenter does. Cut a whole proof point or unnecessary setup before squeezing sentences. Repeat until it fits comfortably without rushing. For recordings, trim dead time without implying performance the work cannot deliver.

## Capture and create

For non-live demos, help obtain the screenshots or recordings needed for the selected proof points. Use available capture tools and supplied assets; otherwise give a precise shot list: starting state, action, result, framing, and what to highlight. Capture only the necessary path, enlarge the relevant area, remove distractions, and use safe demo data. For live demos, prepare the starting state and a screenshot or recording fallback.

Create the requested collateral using the appropriate available skill or tools. Keep this skill focused on the demo; delegate file-format mechanics to those capabilities. Do not stop at an outline when the user wants a finished artifact. If capture or creation is unavailable, say exactly what is missing and what remains to finish. Never imply a planned shot was captured.

Use as few visible words as possible. Prefer screenshots, short clips, arrows, highlights, and brief labels. Give each visual one job. Keep narration in separate speaker notes or a voiceover script, not paragraphs on slides. For unaccompanied posts or documents, retain just enough captioning to make the evidence understandable without a presenter. Check annotations and labels are legible at the intended viewing size.

Deliver the artifact with only the supporting notes, capture gaps, and timing information needed to use it. Drafting materials does not authorise posting or sending them.
