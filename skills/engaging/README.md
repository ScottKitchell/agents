# engaging

Crafts writing or speech that moves a reader: they feel seen, understood, and cared for, and they experience the message instead of being told it. It loads automatically when a piece needs to change how someone feels or acts.

## Why it exists

Agents default to telling. They state the conclusion ("Now is the time to act"), summarise the feeling, and close with a call to action. Readers understand it and feel nothing. This skill makes the agent plan a piece like a script or speech, draft for feeling, and edit until every sentence earns its place.

It sits beside `human-writing`. That skill makes routine communication clear and efficient; this one is for the pieces where engagement is the point. Its voice rules still apply here.

## Research behind it

Practitioners from very different fields converge on the same moves:

- **Arc between what is and what could be, with the audience as hero.** Speech design from [Nancy Duarte's study of great talks](https://www.duarte.com/resources/secret-structure-great-talks/).
- **"But" and "therefore", not "and then".** Trey Parker and Matt Stone's beat test for [South Park scripts](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/04/tony-zhou-storytelling-video-essay-f-fake-therefore-but-and-then).
- **Anecdote, bait, and reflection.** Ira Glass's [building blocks of storytelling](https://www.storycenter.org/storycenter-blog/blog/2013/7/1/ira-glass-on-storytelling): keep raising and answering questions, then say what it means.
- **Every sentence pulls into the next.** Joseph Sugarman's ["slippery slide"](https://larslofgren.com/joseph-sugarman/) from direct-response copywriting.
- **Feeling seen.** Motivational interviewing's [complex reflections](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039097), which guess at the meaning beneath what someone says, predict change better than simple ones, and [matching the person's language style](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5018199) predicts perceived empathy.
- **Stories lower resistance.** [Narrative transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology)) research finds absorbed readers counter-argue less. Corny or inauthentic stories [trigger their own resistance](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/AppelAffectiveResistance.pdf), hence the "keep it true" rule.
- **One person over statistics.** The [identifiable victim effect](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head) from fundraising research. A [preregistered replication](https://stafforini.com/works/small-2007-sympathy-callousness-impact/) failed to reproduce it, so the skill treats it as practitioner wisdom, not law.
- **Commands backfire.** [Psychological reactance](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/) research in health messaging: controlling language like "you must" reduces persuasion, especially with repeated exposure.
- **Edit as a first-time reader.** George Saunders's [P/N meter](https://advicetowriters.com/advice/wheres-the-needle): reread the whole piece each pass and revise wherever attention dips.

These are design precedents, not evidence that the skill improves agent output.

## Iterating

- Give a fresh agent a real brief with and without the skill: a wellbeing nudge to someone who keeps putting themselves last, a short internal speech, a fundraising paragraph. Check the "with" version shows rather than tells, never invents a real-seeming person, and has a clear arc even at three sentences.
- Check it asks about the reader or for real stories when they're missing, instead of inventing them.
- Trigger checks: "make this launch email more compelling", "write a toast for my sister's wedding", "help this appeal land emotionally" should load it. A Slack status update, a PR description, and an error message should not.
- Watch for overcorrection: purple prose, forced stories in short pieces, or rhetorical questions creeping back in.
