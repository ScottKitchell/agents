# Engaging

Treat every piece, whatever its length, like a short film: one point, a planned arc, and every line earning its place. Expect many drafts. The first one is raw material.

## 1. Know the reader

Before writing, answer these about the person reading:

- What moment are they in right now, and what are they feeling?
- What do they already believe, want, and fear about this topic? What have they tried?
- What words would they use to describe their own situation?
- What do they need to feel, and what might they then do, by the end?

Take answers from the task, the user, and any real material available (messages, interviews, research). If the reader or the intended feeling is unclear, ask the user. Also ask for real stories, quotes, or details when the piece will depend on them; they beat anything invented.

## 2. Find the one thing

Write one sentence: what the reader should feel, and the belief or action that follows. Everything in the piece serves that sentence. If two ideas compete, pick one and cut or save the other.

## 3. Plan the arc

Outline the beats before drafting. Even three sentences have a shape.

- Open inside the reader's world: a moment, detail, or tension they recognise as theirs. Not a summary, not a thesis.
- Move between what is and what could be. Make the gap felt, then make the bridge across it feel possible.
- Make the reader the hero. You, the product, or the organisation are the guide who helps, never the centre.
- Connect beats with "but" or "therefore", never "and then". If two beats only connect with "and then", one of them is missing a reason to exist.
- Raise a question in the reader's mind with what you reveal and hold back, then answer it, then raise the next. Do this through content, not rhetorical questions.
- After each story or moment, give a brief reflection on what it means. Reveal meaning; don't issue instructions.
- End on an image, a feeling, or the reader's own choice. Not a slogan or a call to "act now".

## 4. Draft for feeling

**Show, don't tell.** Find every sentence that tells the reader what to feel, think, or do ("Now is the time to act", "This is important", "You deserve better") and replace it with something that produces that response: a specific person, a moment, a concrete detail, a consequence they can picture. One person in one moment moves people more than statistics or abstractions. Use numbers only to support a story, not to replace it.

**Make them feel seen.** Describe their experience more precisely than they could themselves: name the feeling beneath what they'd say aloud, the thing they've half-admitted, the effort they've already made. Use their words. Accept them as they are, with no judgement and no implied "should". Care shows as attention to their reality, not as declarations of care.

**Leave them free.** Controlling language ("you must", "you need to", "don't wait") triggers resistance, especially on health and behaviour. Offer the choice as theirs. A story about someone who chose differently invites; a command pushes.

**Keep it true.** Never present an invented person, quote, or event as real. Use real material, frame an illustration plainly as one ("Picture a Tuesday night…"), or leave a marked placeholder for the user to fill. Fake or overwrought stories read as corny and backfire. Understatement usually moves people more than intensity.

## 5. Edit relentlessly

Run each pass over the whole piece, then repeat until a full pass changes nothing.

1. **One thing:** delete anything that doesn't serve the sentence from step 2, however good it is.
2. **Every sentence:** check each one is distinct, valuable on its own, fits the arc, and makes the reader want the next. Cut or rewrite any that fails. Delete whole sentences before trimming words.
3. **Show:** hunt down remaining telling, abstraction, and generic phrasing. Swap the general for the specific.
4. **Seen:** read as the reader. Mark any line where they would feel judged, lectured, sold to, or misunderstood, and fix it.
5. **First-time read:** read it aloud, start to finish, as someone who has never seen it. Note where attention dips, where you stumble, where it feels false. Vary sentence length so the rhythm carries the reader. Fix the dips.

Weigh every word, but keep the voice natural. Engaging writing still avoids mannered prose, manufactured contrasts, dramatic fragments, and rhetorical questions.

## Done

Return the finished piece. Then, briefly, list any placeholders or illustrative stories the user should replace with real material, and any assumption about the reader that would change the piece if wrong.
