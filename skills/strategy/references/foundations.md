# Foundations and worked example

## What comes from where

This skill combines a state-based planning model (current and desired states, pathways, conditional choices) with selected ideas from the sources below. It is a practical synthesis, not an established framework validated as a whole.

- [Richard Rumelt, The perils of bad strategy (2011)](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy). Rumelt's own account of the diagnosis, guiding policy, and coordinated action at the centre of good strategy. It explains why goals, inventories, and task lists alone are insufficient. This article provides background from the author of *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*; it is not a substitute for consulting the full book when making book-specific claims.
- [Deltares, Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways](https://www.deltares.nl/en/expertise/areas-of-expertise/sea-level-rise/dynamic-adaptive-policy-pathways). The method's developers describe alternative sequences, conditions under which an action ceases to work, and monitoring that enables adaptation. Its original climate and infrastructure context has much longer horizons than most startup decisions. Borrow the conditional choices and early signals; do not import its modelling or governance overhead by default.

Backcasting names the user's backwards reasoning from a desired future. Rolling-wave planning names the progressive refinement of nearer work. Their use here, the lightweight representation, and the delegation boundary are design choices for this skill. Stakeholder analysis should inform plausible conditional responses, rather than supply an illusion of precise prediction.

## Example: a small team's customer acquisition strategy

All facts, thresholds, budgets, and actors below are fictional. They illustrate the reasoning, not recommended business benchmarks.

**Desired state:** Within one quarter, the team has a repeatable acquisition route whose observed contribution covers delivery and acquisition costs, without exceeding its available capacity.

**Current state:** Two people can work on acquisition; there are some satisfied customers but no reliable cost or conversion data. One potential partner is interested, without a commitment. The team can afford a small pilot, not a broad launch.

**Diagnosis:** The binding constraint is evidence of a viable acquisition mechanism. Increasing activity before testing conversion and delivery cost could consume the quarter without finding one. This diagnosis is provisional until the existing customer evidence is inspected.

**Backwards map:**

- S3: A route repeats with acceptable economics. Requires S2 **AND** capacity to deliver repeatedly.
- S2: A bounded pilot demonstrates conversion and delivery economics with enough evidence to justify repetition.
- S1a: A partner has agreed to a concrete pilot and the team can measure it; **OR** S1b: a reachable direct audience and measurable offer are ready.
- S0: The current state above.

Partner and direct routes are alternatives for the first pilot. Delivery measurement is a shared prerequisite; label it once rather than treating it as a separate strategic route.

**Forwards test:** Partner interest may never become agreement. The partner might want exclusivity or have no incentive to prioritise the pilot. Direct acquisition avoids that dependency but may cost more and require a capability the team lacks. A successful first sale also does not establish repeatability.

**Guiding policy:** Concentrate on proving one affordable mechanism before expanding acquisition activity.

**Chosen pathway:** First inspect existing customer and cost evidence. Assuming it supports the diagnosis, pursue the partner pilot because access to its audience could reduce the team's initial effort. Keep preparation bounded; do not build integrations ahead of agreement. Reserve capacity for delivery measurement. Defer a broad paid campaign because this team lacks both evidence and budget for it.

**Conditional response:** If the partner has not agreed to a dated pilot within two weeks, move the pilot effort to the direct route, provided a small manual test shows the audience can be reached within the remaining budget. Set that check before spending the full fortnight. If neither route is affordable, revisit the offer or horizon rather than assuming a fallback exists. If delivery economics fail, pause acquisition expansion and revisit the diagnosis.

**Refinement and hand-off:** Refine S0 → S1a into a smaller strategy for securing a pilot agreement: identify a partner sponsor, establish a mutual benefit, and agree scope and measurement. Hand it off once a proposed owner has the partner context, spending boundary, success conditions, and escalation trigger for exclusivity demands. Do not prescribe every email. Keep the shared delivery measurement dependency with the coordinating lead.

**Review:** Pilot results update the diagnosis and subsequent pathway. The immediate plan is concrete; the expansion plan stays coarse until evidence supports it.

### The hand-off, written out

This is the child strategy for S0 → S1a, as a recipient would receive it.

- **Start:** One partner has expressed interest, with no commitment, dates, or named sponsor.
- **Destination:** Within two weeks, the partner has agreed to a dated pilot with a defined scope, and both sides agree how conversion and delivery cost will be measured.
- **Why this matters:** The parent strategy needs evidence of a viable acquisition mechanism within the quarter, and the partner route is the cheapest way to get an audience.
- **Constraints:** No integration work before agreement. No exclusivity. Spend stays within the small pilot budget.
- **Critical assumption:** The partner has an incentive to prioritise the pilot. Check this first, in the opening conversation.
- **Success test:** A written agreement, or an agreed email, that names the pilot dates, scope, and measurement.
- **Escalate if:** The partner asks for exclusivity or spend beyond the boundary, or there is no agreement by day ten, so the parent can trigger the direct-route check.

## Example: an individual's strategy

Fictional. It shows the method at the "quick exploration" depth, as a short brief, with no table or diagram.

**Destination:** Within a year, move from senior engineer to a role leading a small team, in work I find meaningful, without dropping below my current pay. Non-goal: moving cities.

**Diagnosis:** My technical record is strong, but I have little evidence that I can lead others, and no one with hiring authority has seen me do it. Assumption to check: my current company has a team-lead opening within the year. If it doesn't, the constraint shifts from evidence to opportunity.

**Options:**

- Grow into the role inside the current company by leading a project.
- Move to a smaller company where a lead role is open now.

**Choice:** Concentrate on building visible leadership evidence inside the current company, because it is reversible and cheap, and it also strengthens the outside option. I will not start a broad job search yet.

**Signals and responses:** If my manager can't name a path to a lead role by the next review, begin the outside route, since external opportunities take months to arrive. If I lead a project and find I dislike it, revisit the destination.

**Next move:** Ask my manager this week which upcoming project could use a lead, and what they would need to see.
