# Refinement and hand-offs

## Refining a transition

Every chosen action is a transition between two states, so it can be treated as a smaller strategy. Its source state becomes the starting state, its intended result becomes the desired state, and the five steps apply again. The child is a refinement of the parent transition, so keep the parent's rationale and constraints attached to it.

Refine only where a real choice, obstacle, or uncertainty remains. Routine, well-understood actions need ordinary execution planning. Don't set a fixed recursion depth, and don't expand every hypothetical branch.

Detail the work close enough to act on and leave distant or uncertain transitions coarse (**rolling-wave planning**). Pilot results, for example, should reshape the later pathway, so don't plan it in detail before they arrive.

## When to stop

Stop when the next section can be handed to a team, person, or agent who could pick it up and choose how. For an individual's own strategy, the recipient is the person themselves: stop at the point where they know what to do next and what would change the plan.

## What a hand-off contains

- **Starting and desired state:** conditions, in checkable terms.
- **Parent rationale and constraints:** why this transition is part of the strategy, and what must keep holding.
- **Evidence and critical assumptions:** what is known, what is assumed, and which assumption would change the approach if false.
- **Boundaries:** budget, time, authority, and what is out of scope.
- **Success test:** the evidence that the desired state has been reached.
- **Escalation signals:** the observed conditions that mean the recipient should come back, for example a demand that crosses a boundary.

Leave the recipient room to choose how. Don't prescribe every step.

A hand-off means the section is ready to delegate. It does not authorise assigning work, contacting people, or committing budget. Owners and dates stay proposals until the user or the accountable person confirms them.

## Feeding back to the parent

If a child strategy reveals a missing capability, an incompatible constraint, or an infeasible transition, revise the affected parent pathway and say what changed. Keep cross-team dependencies at the level where they can be coordinated, such as with a shared lead, so that two child strategies don't each assume the other will cover them.
