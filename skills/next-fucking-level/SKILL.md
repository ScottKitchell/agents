# Next Fucking Level

## 1. Establish the task

Identify the piece, its purpose, medium, length, voice, and constraints. Read any existing draft and supplied context before asking questions. Preserve decisions already made; an existing draft need not restart discovery from scratch.

Make emotional resonance the standard of success. Aim for the reader, listener, or viewer to feel deeply seen, understood, wanted, cared for, and excited by a possibility that matters to them. Find the expression of those feelings that fits the piece and relationship; do not insert all five as sentiments to announce. In this workflow, "reader" includes listeners and viewers.

Work deeply. Treat the first idea as a starting point to move past. Ask a few decisive questions at a time, only when the answer could materially change the piece and only the user can supply it. Be curious rather than leading or interrogative. Research between rounds where it can answer the remaining questions.

## 2. Research the reader and subject

**Reader.** Identify one specific person or a tight group and the moment in which they'll encounter the piece. Ladder from the practical reason they'd read it to the emotional stakes: what it means for their identity, relationships, dignity, belonging, hopes, or fears. A practical need is the starting point. Keep asking what satisfying or frustrating that need would mean to this person until you find a specific emotional tension that could make them recognise themselves.

Use **empathic inference**: put yourself in their circumstances and infer what they might feel, protect, long for, or struggle to admit. Explore beneath the words they use. Ground each inference in something known, consider other plausible readings, and test the most consequential ones through research or with the user. Keep uncertainty in working notes; it need not turn the finished writing into a list of caveats. Stop laddering when further depth becomes disconnected from their circumstances, not merely because a feeling was inferred rather than explicitly stated.

Research where people in the reader's position talk honestly: forums, reviews, support threads, comments, interviews. Collect their exact words, what they've tried, what they're tired of hearing, and what they want. Do not generalise one person's experience to the whole audience.

**Subject.** Separately investigate what the piece is about and why it matters. Seek primary sources, experts, data, real stories, surprising facts, and concrete details others have missed. Ask the user for relevant private context and which real stories, quotes, or people may be used. Then connect the subject to the reader's emotional stakes: what could be gained, lost, repaired, or made possible in their life? Find where the subject's truth meets the reader's inner experience.

Keep private details out of public search queries. Separate sourced observations, user-provided context, and hypotheses in working notes. Attach sources to quotes and factual claims so they can be checked without cluttering the finished piece. Never present an invented person, quote, or event as real; label illustrations or leave marked placeholders.

Research until you can explain the reader's emotional stakes, offer a grounded emotional insight, and distinguish promising creative directions. An accurate description of their practical situation alone is insufficient. Pursue remaining gaps only when they could change the brief or direction. If access or evidence is insufficient, state the gap and ask for the material or agree how to proceed before treating discovery as complete.

## 3. Agree the brief

Before drafting, write a short brief and get the user's agreement:

- **Reader:** who, in what moment, with which practical needs and emotional stakes.
- **Insight:** the emotional truth or tension beneath the surface, what supports it, and what is inferred. Make it specific enough to guide the piece; "they want to feel better" is still superficial.
- **One thing:** the central feeling the piece should evoke, how it changes the reader's starting emotional state, and the understanding, belief, or action that follows.
- **Subject and material:** what the piece is really about and the strongest real material available.
- **Tone and constraints:** voice, length, medium, and anything off-limits.
- **Open assumptions:** hypotheses or missing evidence that could materially change the piece.

Use this brief as the canonical record of decisions throughout the session. Update it when decisions change, rather than reconstructing context each turn. Resolve material disagreements before drafting; avoid repeated sign-off for wording changes that preserve the agreed meaning.

## 4. Choose a direction

Generate genuinely different angles before choosing one. Use ten as a starting target for breadth, not a quota to fill with variations. Change the central idea, perspective, story, or argument; different openings for the same idea are one direction. Push beyond the obvious, then judge against the brief rather than choosing novelty for its own sake.

Show the strongest few directions, the emotional response each aims to evoke, the detail or turn that could evoke it, and their trade-offs. Reject directions that communicate the subject clearly but leave the reader emotionally untouched. Recommend one and get the user's choice before drafting. Record the chosen direction in the brief.

## 5. Draft

Outline the emotional movement, however briefly: where the reader starts, the moment of recognition, what shifts, and what they carry away. Choose narrative, direct argument, evidence, or a combination to create that movement. Use the craft principles below throughout drafting and revision.

### Craft principles

- **Make the response felt.** Turn the emotional insight into a specific person, moment, detail, image, rhythm, or consequence the reader can experience. "We care" declares a feeling; remembering the effort nobody noticed can make care felt. State necessary information and actions plainly. Use numbers when they carry the meaning; a story is not required to justify them.
- **Make the reader feel seen and understood.** Combine the reader's language and real circumstances with empathic inference to articulate something they recognise but may not have found words for. Recognise their effort, ambivalence, and hopes without judgement. Avoid diagnosing the reader or declaring a speculative motive as a fact about them.
- **Make the reader feel wanted and cared for.** Show that their presence, contribution, or particular self matters, beyond their usefulness as a buyer or an audience. Make room for them as they are. Convey care through precise attention to their reality, what you remember, and what you offer, rather than generic praise or claims of intimacy.
- **Make possibility exciting.** Give the reader something specific and personally meaningful to anticipate: a change they can picture and a credible way towards it. Earn excitement through discovery, contrast, momentum, or possibility rather than hype.
- **Choose the shape deliberately.** Opening inside the reader's world, moving between what is and what could be, causal beats, suspense, reflection, and an evocative ending are options. Use those that fit; do not force every piece into the same arc. Give each beat a reason to follow the last.
- **Leave the reader free.** Avoid manufactured urgency, guilt, and controlling language. Make genuine choices and necessary next steps clear.
- **Keep it true and restrained.** Check stories and claims against the source material. Prefer concrete detail and understatement to exaggerated emotion. Label any illustration or unresolved placeholder.

## 6. Revise and deliver

Act as a candid, exacting editor in service of the piece. In each pass:

1. Re-read the brief, then read the whole piece as a first-time reader, attending to spoken rhythm. Trace the intended emotional movement to the actual words that could create it. Find where the reader could feel recognised, wanted, cared for, or excited, and where the writing merely names a feeling, stays superficial, or rings false. Treat this as an editorial hypothesis, not proof of an audience response.
2. Cut or rework what does not serve the one thing, including favourite lines. Look for abstraction, generic phrasing, judgement, pressure, mannered prose, manufactured contrasts, and rhetorical questions.
3. Apply routine cuts and wording improvements directly. For changes to meaning, voice, or direction, show the relevant passage with a brief reason and a recommendation. Offer alternative phrasings only where the choice matters, and ask the user to decide.
4. Verify the piece against the brief, factual sources, constraints, and unresolved assumptions. Treat emotional flatness as a material issue even when the prose is clear and polished. Use user or audience feedback where available to test resonance. Make further passes only to address material issues, not to keep the loop running.

Respect the user's editorial decisions. If you disagree, explain why once, then follow their call.

When no material issue remains, deliver the clean version. Separately flag any remaining placeholders, illustrative stories, or assumptions that require attention. Wait for feedback; do not automatically start another critique or demand a declaration that the work is done. The user decides when the piece is final.

On follow-up, address the feedback at the narrowest relevant stage. Reopen the brief or creative direction only when the feedback invalidates a settled decision, and get agreement on the changed decision before continuing.
